1D box · For G1 and R1

Two wavelengths, two jobs

Picture an electron confined to a segment of length L. In the ideal model, the potential is zero inside and the walls are impenetrable. The electron’s wave function must vanish at both ends. Only standing waves with a whole number of half-wavelengths fit, so the allowed energies are discrete.

Keep the two separate. The standing electron wave tells you which energy levels exist. The photon wavelength tells you how much energy the light supplies.

Use it on paper

From level filling to a wavelength

  1. Fill the levels. In the independent-electron model, start at n = 1 and place at most two electrons, with opposite spins, in each spatial level. For these closed-shell examples, the HOMO is the highest occupied molecular orbital and the LUMO is the lowest unoccupied one. The box levels stand in for those orbitals.
  2. Find the gap. Get the two energies from the box formula, using L in metres, then subtract the lower energy from the higher one. Subtract the squared indices, rather than squaring their difference.
    En=n2h28meL2
    ΔE=ELUMO−EHOMO
    Here n is the level index, h is the Planck constant, and me is the electron mass. With SI inputs the gap is in joules per transition, not joules per mole.
  3. Get the photon wavelength. Set the photon energy equal to the model gap. With the speed of light c in m/s and the gap in joules, the wavelength is in metres. Multiply by 109 to convert it to nanometres.
    λphoton=hcΔE
    A smaller gap means a longer wavelength. Round only after the final conversion.

Optional depth

Why it works

Where does the factor of 8 come from?

The boundary conditions give L = nλdB/2. With the de Broglie relation, the momentum magnitude for either travelling-wave component is |p| = h/λdB = nh/(2L). The two components have opposite momenta, but the same kinetic energy p2/(2me). Squaring the 2 from the box condition and multiplying by the 2 in the kinetic-energy denominator gives 8.

Setting n = 0 gives the identically zero sine wave, not a physical state. Negative n merely changes the overall sign of the wave function. The first distinct state is therefore n = 1.

Where can the model mislead us?

In a conjugated chain, the π electrons are delocalised. The box replaces that molecular environment with a flat potential and uses an assumed effective length. It leaves out details such as bond-length alternation and electron–electron interactions. At fixed level indices, the gap scales as 1/L2, so even a modest change in the assumed length affects the wavelength.

Use the model to reason about confinement and trends, not as an exact description of an absorption spectrum. In R1, compare your predicted wavelength with the reference value and ask whether they fall in the same spectral region.

Core idea · For G2 and C1

Particle in a 2D box

An electron confined to a rectangle has a standing-wave pattern in each direction. Count the half-waves separately: nx along the horizontal side Lx and ny along the vertical side Ly. Both indices start at 1. The wall in either direction forces the wave function to zero along that edge.

Enx,ny =h28me (nx2Lx2 +ny2Ly2)

Each term is a 1D confinement energy. Putting the same number of half-waves into a shorter side costs more energy, because the wavelength is shorter.

Degenerate means same energy, not same state. The square-box patterns shown here are two distinct spatial states. Each can hold up to two electrons of opposite spin.

Calculation route

  1. Choose the formula for the shape. For a square, set Lx = Ly and factor out the common energy scale used in G2 and C1. In a rectangle, keep the two side lengths separate.
  2. Rank states by energy. Square each index separately. Compare the resulting energies, keeping equal-energy states distinct. The common scale named E0 in C1 is a prefactor, not the ground-state energy of the 2D box.
  3. Fill from the lowest energy up. Count two spin states per spatial state. When two spatial states have the same energy, that energy can accommodate four electrons, not two.
Why do the energies add?

The 2D wave function is the product of an x sine and a y sine. The x part of the kinetic-energy operator acts only on the x sine, and the y part acts only on the y sine. Each produces its own 1D energy contribution, so the two contributions add.

A direction with index n has n − 1 interior zeros, not n. In two dimensions each such zero extends into a nodal line. The box walls are not counted as interior nodes.

What does the square model say about benzene?

The square in the Try-Test is a way to practise ranking states and counting electrons at degenerate energies. Real benzene is not square. The model is not a molecular-orbital calculation for benzene.

Core idea · For C2

Hydrogen wave functions

For hydrogen, it is useful to describe the electron’s position by its distance from the nucleus and its direction. The orbital wave function separates into two factors. The radial factor depends only on distance. The angular factor depends only on direction.

ψn,ℓ,m (r,θ,φ) =Rn,ℓ(r) ·Yℓ,m(θ,φ)

Which indices go in which table?

n · Principal quantum number
The number in the label, starting at 1. It is used for the radial factor.
ℓ · Orbital angular momentum
The letter gives the index: s = 0, p = 1, d = 2, f = 3. Use ℓ in both tables. It can range from 0 to n − 1.
m · Magnetic quantum number
Also written mℓ. Use the given integer, including its sign, in the angular table. The allowed values run from −ℓ to +ℓ.

The table-lookup route

  1. Read the label. Write down n, ℓ, and m before looking for either factor.
  2. Pick two rows. Match (n, ℓ) in the radial table and (ℓ, m) in the angular table. Changing m does not change the radial row. If a row uses paired signs, choose the signs together as printed in that table.
  3. Multiply, then simplify. Use the definition of the scaled distance ρ that accompanies the table. Keep any angular factors, signs and phase factors. At this point you are constructing ψ, not squaring it.
Worked example · Assemble a 2s orbital

For 2s, n = 2 and ℓ = 0, so m can only be 0. The radial table gives R2,0 = a0−3/2(2 − ρ)e−ρ/2/√8. The angular table gives Y0,0 = 1/√(4π). For hydrogen in the fixed-nucleus approximation, ρ = 2r/(n a0), where a0 is the Bohr radius. For this state, ρ = r/a0.

ψ2,0,0= 142πa03/2 (2−ρ)e−ρ/2

Read the result. There are no angles in the expression, so the orbital is spherically symmetric. It vanishes on the sphere ρ = 2, a radial node. That does not mean the wave function is zero at every radius.

What does the wave function tell us?

ψ is a probability amplitude. It can have a sign or be complex. The probability density is |ψ|2, always real and non-negative. The probability in a small volume is approximately this density times the volume. The probability in a thin spherical shell instead includes the radial volume factor r2. For a normalised angular factor, its radial density is r2|R(r)|2.

A factor such as eimφ has magnitude one for real angles. It can drop out of the density of that state, but it belongs in ψ. C2 asks for the wave function.

Core idea · For R2

Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle

A state can have a tightly confined position or a narrowly defined momentum, but it cannot have both spreads arbitrarily small. R2 asks for a lower bound on the velocity spread for a given position spread. It does not ask for the actual spread or the particle’s speed.

Δx·Δpx≥ℏ2=h4π

Here Δx and Δpx are standard deviations for position and momentum along the same axis. Think of the spread of results when measuring identically prepared systems. This spread belongs to the quantum state and is present even with an ideal instrument. It is not an error bar from an imperfect measurement.

Calculation route

  1. Identify the given spread. Use the Δx supplied as a standard deviation. A box length, a diameter or a quoted measurement tolerance is not automatically Δx.
  2. Use SI units. Write Δx in metres and the mass m in kilograms. For a fixed mass in the nonrelativistic approximation, px = m vx, so Δpx = m Δvx.
  3. Isolate the lower bound. Divide by the positive product m Δx and keep the inequality.
    Δvx≥h4πmΔx
    The result is in m/s because J·s divided by kg·m gives m/s. If only the mass changes, a larger mass gives a smaller lower bound.
Worked example · An electron with a 0.500 nm position spread

Take Δx = 5.00 × 10−10 m and me = 9.109 × 10−31 kg. With h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s:

Δvx≥ 6.626×10−344π(9.109×10−31)(5.00×10−10)

The lower bound is 1.16 × 105 m/s. It is a bound on the spread of velocity values, not a statement about the mean velocity. Halving Δx doubles the bound at the same mass.

Why does a narrow wave packet need more momenta?

A single plane wave has a definite momentum and no localised envelope. To make a localised packet, waves with different wave numbers interfere. A narrower packet needs a broader range of wave numbers, and px = ℏkx maps that range to momentum. The inequality gives a minimum for the product of the two spreads, not a rule that one spread must double whenever the other is halved.

Core idea · For H1

Particle in a 3D box

Adding a third direction to the 2D box adds a third confinement energy. An electron in an ideal rectangular box has a standing-wave factor along each of the three edges. The wave function must vanish at all six walls.

Why is the lowest energy not zero?

In each direction, the lowest allowed mode has n = 1, with one half-wave between opposite walls. Setting any index to 0 makes its sine factor zero everywhere. The entire wave function would then vanish and could not describe an electron.

The ground state therefore has nx = ny = nz = 1. With the potential inside the box defined as zero, its energy is positive. This is the zero-point energy.

Calculation route

Enx,ny,nz =h28me (nx2Lx2 +ny2Ly2 +nz2Lz2)
  1. Choose the state. For the ground state, put 1 in all three numerators.
  2. Convert before squaring. Write each edge length in metres. Square the lengths separately and add their reciprocals.
  3. Apply the common factor. Multiply the sum by h2/(8me). The factor is in J·m2, so the result is in joules. The shortest side contributes the most to the ground-state energy.
Worked example · A different rectangular box

Take an electron in a box with sides 1.50 nm, 2.50 nm and 4.00 nm. In the ground state, the three terms are 1/(1.50 × 10−9 m)2, 1/(2.50 × 10−9 m)2 and 1/(4.00 × 10−9 m)2. Their sum is approximately 6.67 × 1017 m−2.

With h = 6.626 × 10−34 J·s and me = 9.109 × 10−31 kg, the common factor is about 6.02 × 10−38 J·m2. Multiplying before rounding gives 4.02 × 10−20 J.

Quick checks · A cube and a change of scale

In a cube all three ground-state contributions are equal. The total is three times the 1D ground-state energy for that edge length. Doubling all three sides makes the energy one quarter as large. Doubling just one side changes only that side’s contribution.
