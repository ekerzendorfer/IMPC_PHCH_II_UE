Particle in a 1D box

How confinement gives electrons discrete energies, and how a jump between those energies sets the wavelength of absorbed light.

The model Calculation route Why it works Lecture references

Core idea · For G1 and R1

Two wavelengths, two jobs

Picture an electron confined to a segment of length L. In the ideal model, the potential is zero inside and the walls are impenetrable. The electron’s wave function must vanish at both ends. Only standing waves with a whole number of half-wavelengths fit, so the allowed energies are discrete.

Keep the two separate. The standing electron wave tells you which energy levels exist. The photon wavelength tells you how much energy the light supplies.

Use it on paper

From level filling to a wavelength

  1. Fill the levels. In the independent-electron model, start at n = 1 and place at most two electrons, with opposite spins, in each spatial level. For these closed-shell examples, the HOMO is the highest occupied molecular orbital and the LUMO is the lowest unoccupied one. The box levels stand in for those orbitals.
  2. Find the gap. Get the two energies from the box formula, using L in metres, then subtract the lower energy from the higher one. Subtract the squared indices, rather than squaring their difference.
    En=n2h28meL2
    ΔE=ELUMO−EHOMO
    Here n is the level index, h is the Planck constant, and me is the electron mass. With SI inputs the gap is in joules per transition, not joules per mole.
  3. Get the photon wavelength. Set the photon energy equal to the model gap. With the speed of light c in m/s and the gap in joules, the wavelength is in metres. Multiply by 109 to convert it to nanometres.
    λphoton=hcΔE
    A smaller gap means a longer wavelength. Round only after the final conversion.

Optional depth

Why it works

Where does the factor of 8 come from?

The boundary conditions give L = nλdB/2. With the de Broglie relation, the momentum magnitude for either travelling-wave component is |p| = h/λdB = nh/(2L). The two components have opposite momenta, but the same kinetic energy p2/(2me). Squaring the 2 from the box condition and multiplying by the 2 in the kinetic-energy denominator gives 8.

Setting n = 0 gives the identically zero sine wave, not a physical state. Negative n merely changes the overall sign of the wave function. The first distinct state is therefore n = 1.

Where can the model mislead us?

In a conjugated chain, the π electrons are delocalised. The box replaces that molecular environment with a flat potential and uses an assumed effective length. It leaves out details such as bond-length alternation and electron–electron interactions. At fixed level indices, the gap scales as 1/L2, so even a modest change in the assumed length affects the wavelength.

Use the model to reason about confinement and trends, not as an exact description of an absorption spectrum. In R1, compare your predicted wavelength with the reference value and ask whether they fall in the same spectral region.
