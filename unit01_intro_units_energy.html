Guided

G1 · One gas, two consistent unit systems

A rigid vessel with a volume of 2.50 L contains nitrogen at 25.0 °C and an absolute pressure of 1.20 bar. Assume ideal-gas behaviour.

Calculate the amount of nitrogen in moles. First keep the pressure in bar and the volume in litres. Then verify your result using SI units.

Before calculating. Which value of $R$ lets us keep bar and litres? What must we convert in both calculations?

Step 1 · Set up the calculation

Rearrange $pV=nRT$ to $n=pV/(RT)$. Use absolute temperature. For $25.0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ use $T=298.15\,\mathrm K$ in the calculation and round the final answer only.

Step 2 · Work in litres and bar

Choose $R=0.0831446\,\mathrm{L\,bar\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

$$n=\frac{(1.20\,\mathrm{bar})(2.50\,\mathrm L)}{(0.0831446\,\mathrm{L\,bar\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}})(298.15\,\mathrm K)}=\boxed{0.121\,\mathrm{mol}}.$$

Calculator: Select the gas constant with L and bar in the constants menu, then enter 1.20*2.50/(R_Lbar*(25.0+273.15)).

Step 3 · Verify in SI units

Use $p=1.20\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $V=2.50\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ and $R=8.31446\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

$$n=\frac{(1.20\times10^5\,\mathrm{Pa})(2.50\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3})}{(8.31446\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}})(298.15\,\mathrm K)}=\boxed{0.121\,\mathrm{mol}}.$$

$1\,\mathrm{Pa\,m^3}=1\,\mathrm J$, whereas $1\,\mathrm{bar\,L}=100\,\mathrm J$. The two values of $R$ represent the same constant in different units.

Pause and explain. A student gets $0.00121\,\mathrm{mol}$. Which units did they mix?

G2 · From one photon to one mole of photons

Light has a wavelength of 600.0 nm. Calculate the energy of one photon in joules and the energy of one mole of these photons in kilojoules per mole. Use $E_\gamma=hc/\lambda$ and the constants below.

$h=6.626\,070\,15\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $c=299\,792\,458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $N_\mathrm A=6.022\,140\,76\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$.

Before calculating. With $c$ in metres per second, what unit should we use for the wavelength? Does $hc/\lambda$ refer to one photon or a mole of photons?

Step 1 · Calculate the energy of one photon
$$\lambda=600.0\,\mathrm{nm}=6.000\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m,\qquad E_\gamma=\frac{hc}{\lambda}=3.310743\ldots\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J.$$

The units give $(\mathrm{J\,s})(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})/\mathrm m=\mathrm J$. The rounded result for one photon is $3.311\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$.

Calculator: Enter h*c/(600e-9). Insert $h$ and $c$ from the constants menu. EE enters the e in e-9. Store the unrounded result in M1.

Step 2 · Scale up to a mole

Multiply by $N_\mathrm A$ to get joules per mole, then convert to kilojoules per mole.

$$E_{\mathrm{mol}}=N_\mathrm A E_\gamma=1.993776\ldots\times10^5\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}=\boxed{199.4\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}.$$

Calculator: Clear the expression, recall M1 and append *N_A/1000. Keep the unit and the molar basis in your written answer.

Show discussion solution · C1

In metres, the nuclear radius is $5\times10^{-15}$, the bond length is $1.50\times10^{-10}$ and the light wavelength is $5.50\times10^{-7}$, in increasing order. Since $150\,\mathrm{pm}=0.150\,\mathrm{nm}$, the ratio is $550/0.150\approx3.7\times10^3$. The closest choice is 4,000. Both the prefix and the number in front of it matter.

Concept · reserve

C2 · Can the units tell us what $h\nu$ is?

The unit of Planck's constant $h$ is J·s. The unit of frequency $\nu$ is Hz, or s⁻¹. Determine the unit of $h\nu$ and say what kind of quantity it represents.

The discussion solution is released after in-class practice.

Show discussion solution · C2

$(\mathrm{J\,s})(\mathrm{s^{-1}})=\mathrm J$. The product is an energy. The units alone do not tell us that it refers to one photon; that comes from the physical relation $E_\gamma=h\nu$.

Step B · Determine the volume of argon.

Show complete solution · R1

First convert the mass to amount: $n=m/M=(2.00\,\mathrm g)/(39.95\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}})=0.0500626\ldots\,\mathrm{mol}$. For $20.0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ use $T=293.15\,\mathrm K$. Choose $R=8.31446\,\mathrm{L\,kPa\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

$$V=\frac{nRT}{p}=\frac{(0.0500626\ldots\,\mathrm{mol})(8.31446\,\mathrm{L\,kPa\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}})(293.15\,\mathrm K)}{98.0\,\mathrm{kPa}}=1.245\ldots\,\mathrm L\approx\boxed{1.25\,\mathrm L}.$$

Check the units: $1\,\mathrm{kPa\,L}=1\,\mathrm J$. Thus the kPa–L entry of $R$ has the same numerical value as the SI entry. A consistent calculation in Pa and m³, followed by conversion to litres, is also valid.

Optional challenge

H1 · From combustion data to a mystery molecule

A 0.1341 g sample of a pure compound X containing only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen is completely burned. It produces 0.2200 g of CO₂ and 0.09005 g of H₂O. The molar mass of X is 134.13 g mol⁻¹.

  1. Determine the empirical formula and then the molecular formula.
  2. Explain why the oxygen content cannot be calculated by simply counting all oxygen atoms in the products.
  3. After finding the formula, use the optional clues to suggest a structure. What can the combustion data not tell you about the structure?

Use $M(\mathrm C)=12.011$, $M(\mathrm H)=1.008$ and $M(\mathrm O)=15.999$, in g·mol⁻¹.

Optional help · OpenStax empirical and molecular formulas

Optional structure clues · open after finding the formula

One candidate is the sugar whose residue occurs in DNA. Compare your formula with that of D-ribose, $\mathrm{C_5H_{10}O_5}$. How many oxygen atoms are missing?

Second clue · locate the missing oxygen

In the Fischer projection of open-chain D-ribose, start at the aldehyde carbon (C1). The hydroxyl groups at C2, C3 and C4 point right. At C2, replace its –OH by H and keep the arrangement at C3 and C4. Sketch the result. The clues, not the combustion data, specify this structure.

The optional solution is released separately.

Show complete solution · H1

One mole of CO₂ contains one mole of C atoms and one mole of H₂O contains two moles of H atoms. Thus $n(\mathrm C)=0.2200/44.009\approx0.004999\,\mathrm{mol}$ and $n(\mathrm H)=2(0.09005/18.015)\approx0.009997\,\mathrm{mol}$.

$m(\mathrm C)\approx0.06004\,\mathrm g$ and $m(\mathrm H)\approx0.01008\,\mathrm g$. The difference from $0.1341\,\mathrm g$ is $m(\mathrm O)\approx0.06398\,\mathrm g$, so $n(\mathrm O)\approx0.003999\,\mathrm{mol}$. Oxygen in the products comes partly from the oxygen supplied for combustion.

Divide by $n(\mathrm O)$ to obtain roughly $1.25:2.50:1$. Multiply all entries by four to obtain $5:10:4$, so the empirical formula is $\mathrm{C_5H_{10}O_4}$. Its formula mass corresponds to $134.13\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$. The multiplier is one, so the molecular formula is also $\boxed{\mathrm{C_5H_{10}O_4}}$.

Structure. The clues point to 2-deoxy-D-ribose. The open-chain connectivity is CHO–CH₂–CH(OH)–CH(OH)–CH₂OH, retaining the D-ribose configuration at C3 and C4. In DNA the sugar residue is cyclic. Combustion data and molar mass give a molecular formula, not a unique connectivity, ring form or stereochemistry.

H2 · Spot the mistake and fix it

Decide whether each statement is correct or wrong. Correct every wrong statement and justify your answer in one sentence. The checks are for practice only.

1. The calculator entry 5.0e-7 means $5.0\times10^{-7}$.

2. Since $1\,\mathrm{cm}=10^{-2}\,\mathrm m$, it follows that $1\,\mathrm{cm^3}=10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m^3}$.

3. A wavelength of $5.00\times10^2\,\mathrm{nm}$ corresponds to a wavenumber of $2.00\times10^4\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$.

4. To convert the energy of one photon from joules to electronvolts, first multiply by the Avogadro constant.

5. For pressure in kPa and volume in L, we can use $R=8.31446\,\mathrm{L\,kPa\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

6. For a gas at $20.0\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, the temperature to enter in $pV=nRT$ is $20.0\,\mathrm K$.

Extra challenge for early finishers

A student reports 2.50 eV per photon and 120 kJ mol⁻¹ for the same light. Can both values be correct? Show a short calculation without finding the wavelength.

Explanations are released with the optional-solutions code.

Show explanations and extra challenge solution · H2
  1. Correct. In this context e-7 means “times ten to the power of minus seven”.
  2. Wrong. Cube the length conversion factor, so $1\,\mathrm{cm^3}=(10^{-2}\,\mathrm m)^3=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3}$.
  3. Correct. $500\,\mathrm{nm}=5.00\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{cm}$. Its reciprocal is $2.00\times10^4\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$.
  4. Wrong. Divide the photon energy in joules by $1.602\,176\,634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J/eV}$. Multiplying by $N_\mathrm A$ gives a value per mole instead.
  5. Correct. $1\,\mathrm{kPa\,L}=(10^3\,\mathrm{Pa})(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3})=1\,\mathrm J$, so the numerical value is the same in J and in kPa·L.
  6. Wrong. In $pV=nRT$, use $20.0+273.15=293.15$ K in the calculation, not 20.0 K.

Extra challenge: Convert 2.50 eV to joules per photon, multiply by $N_\mathrm A$ and convert to kJ: $2.50(1.602\,176\,634\times10^{-19})N_\mathrm A/1000\approx241\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. The two reported values are not consistent.
