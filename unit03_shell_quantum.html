G1 · Hexatriene in a 1D box

Theory · Particle in a 1D box

Before calculating, distinguish the electron’s standing wave from the light it absorbs. L22, slides 19–20.

Model 1,3,5-hexatriene as six π electrons in a 1D box of assumed effective length $L=0.730\,\mathrm{nm}$. Find the HOMO and LUMO, their energy difference, and the absorption wavelength. For comparison, OpenStax lists an absorption maximum of 258 nm.

Step a · Fill the levels
Step (a) – Orbital Filling ($6\text{ }\pi\text{-Electrons}$):

With 2 electrons ($\uparrow\downarrow$) per level, $N = 6$ electrons fill the lowest three levels $n = 1, 2, 3$:

$$n_{\text{HOMO}} = \frac{N}{2} = \mathbf{3}, \qquad n_{\text{LUMO}} = n_{\text{HOMO}} + 1 = \mathbf{4}$$
Step b · Calculate the gap
Step (b) – HOMO $\to$ LUMO Excitation Energy $\Delta E$:

Converting $L = 0.730\text{ nm} = 0.730 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m}$:

$$\Delta E = E_4 - E_3 = (4^2 - 3^2)\frac{h^2}{8 m_e L^2} = 7 \cdot \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J s})^2}{8 \cdot (9.109 \times 10^{-31}\text{ kg}) \cdot (0.730 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m})^2} = \mathbf{7.91 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}} \quad (\mathbf{4.94\text{ eV}})$$
Step c · Find the wavelength
Step (c) – Absorption Wavelength $\lambda$:
$$\lambda = \frac{h \cdot c}{\Delta E} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}\text{ J s} \cdot 2.9979 \times 10^8\text{ m s}^{-1}}{7.914 \times 10^{-19}\text{ J}} = 2.510 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m} = \mathbf{251\text{ nm}}$$

The calculation is close to the reference value for this assumed length. That agreement alone does not establish how well the model generalises. Try the same method in R1.

R1 · From β-carotene occupancy to wavelength

Theory · Particle in a 1D box

Use the calculation route to find the level indices, the energy gap and the photon wavelength. L22, slide 20 and PDF p. 83, Exercise 6.

Treat the 22 delocalised π electrons of β-carotene as independent electrons in a one-dimensional box with effective length $L=2.94\,\mathrm{nm}$. The reference absorption wavelength in L22 is $497\,\mathrm{nm}$. Write down your reasoning before each check.

(a) Find the quantum numbers of the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied levels.

(b) Calculate the energy gap between those two levels.

(c) Predict the absorption wavelength.

Compare your wavelength with the reference value. Would the box model put the absorption in the same spectral region? What did the modelling assumption leave out?

The complete solution is released after in-class practice.

Show complete solution · R1

(a) Two electrons per level fill $n=1$ to $11$. Therefore $n_{\mathrm{HOMO}}=11$ and $n_{\mathrm{LUMO}}=12$.

(b) Use $L=2.94\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm m$ and subtract the two energies:

$$\Delta E=(12^2-11^2)\frac{h^2}{8m_eL^2}=1.60\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J\approx1.00\,\mathrm{eV}.$$

(c) With the unrounded gap, $\lambda=hc/\Delta E\approx 1.24\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm m$, or about $1239\,\mathrm{nm}$.

Model check. The prediction is in the near infrared, whereas $497\,\mathrm{nm}$ lies in the visible. Compared with G1 the model captures the trend toward longer wavelengths, but it assumes a flat potential and depends strongly on the effective length. It omits the molecular bonding pattern, including bond-length alternation, and electron interactions.

G2 · From a 1D box to a square 2D box

Theory · Particle in a 2D box

Use the rectangle formula to find the common energy scale when both sides are equal. L22, slides 21–22 · T02, Q4 (printed p. 6, PDF p. 8).

Starting from the rectangular 2D box, simplify the expression for $L_x=L_y=L$ and identify the common energy scale $E_0$.

Guided derivation · Square box
Step (b) – Energy Formula in a Square 2D Box ($L_x = L_y = L$):

In 2D, the energy is the sum of the independent $x$- and $y$-motions ($n_1, n_2 \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$):

$$E_{n_1,n_2} = \frac{h^2}{8 m_e}\left(\frac{n_1^2}{L_x^2} + \frac{n_2^2}{L_y^2}\right) \xrightarrow{L_x = L_y = L} \frac{h^2}{8 m_e L^2}(n_1^2 + n_2^2) = \mathbf{E_0 \cdot (n_1^2 + n_2^2)}$$
Optional extension · de Broglie wavelength (L22 slides 10–12, Exercise 2)

An electron starts from rest and is accelerated through 100 V. Using nonrelativistic kinetic energy gives $eU=p^2/(2m_e)$, so $\lambda=h/\sqrt{2m_e eU}\approx123\,\mathrm{pm}$. This predicts $\lambda\propto U^{-1/2}$.

C1 · Degeneracy and electron occupation

Theory · Particle in a 2D box

Use the square–rectangle comparison to distinguish equal energies from identical states before filling the levels. L22, slides 21–22 · T02, Q4 (printed p. 6, PDF p. 8).

Put six noninteracting electrons in a square box. Consider $(n_x,n_y)=(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)$.

(a) Enter the integer coefficients of the three energies in units of the common scale $E_0$.

(b) Explain the equality of two energies, then decide how the electrons are distributed.

The complete solution is released after in-class practice.

Show complete solution · C1

$E_{1,1}=2E_0$ and $E_{1,2}=E_{2,1}=5E_0$. With equal side lengths the energy depends on the sum $n_x^2+n_y^2$, which is unchanged when the indices are exchanged. State $(1,2)$ has no interior node along $x$ and one along $y$. State $(2,1)$ has the reverse pattern. The six electrons occupy the three lowest spatial states in pairs, with opposite spins in each pair.

C2 · Assemble a hydrogen wave function

Theory · Hydrogen wave functions

Use the two-table method to assemble the wave function on paper. L22, slides 25–27 · T02, Q5 (printed p. 7, PDF p. 9).

For the hydrogen $2p$ orbital with $m=-1$, mark one radial row and one angular row. Then write their product on paper. The check only verifies the row selections.

Radial factor · (n, ℓ)
IndicesFactor
$2(Z/a_0)^{3/2}e^{-\rho/2}$
$(Z/a_0)^{3/2}(2-\rho)e^{-\rho/2}/\sqrt{8}$
$(Z/a_0)^{3/2}\rho e^{-\rho/2}/\sqrt{24}$
Angular factor · (ℓ, m)
IndicesFactor
$1/\sqrt{4\pi}$
$\sqrt{3/(4\pi)}\cos\theta$
$+\sqrt{3/(8\pi)}\sin\theta\,e^{-i\phi}$
$-\sqrt{3/(8\pi)}\sin\theta\,e^{+i\phi}$

For hydrogen use $Z=1$, $a_0$ as the Bohr radius in the fixed-nucleus approximation, and $\rho=2Zr/(na_0)$.

The complete solution is released after in-class practice.

Show complete solution · C2

The indices are $n=2$, $\ell=1$, $m=-1$. Select $R_{2,1}$ and $Y_{1,-1}$. Multiplication gives:

$$\psi_{2,1,-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{24}}\left(\frac{Z}{a_0}\right)^{3/2}\rho e^{-\rho/2} \sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\sin\theta\,e^{-i\phi} =\frac{1}{8\sqrt\pi}\left(\frac{Z}{a_0}\right)^{3/2}\rho e^{-\rho/2}\sin\theta\,e^{-i\phi}.$$

For hydrogen, $Z=1$ and $\rho=r/a_0$ in the approximation used here.

R2 · A lower bound from the uncertainty relation

Theory · Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle

Use the calculation route to turn a position spread into a lower bound on the velocity spread. L22, slide 8, slide 13 (Exercise 3) and slide 41.

For each system use the given $\Delta x$ as a position standard deviation. Enter velocities in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

Complete the last check in R1 to open the first calculation in R2.

(a) Mosquito · $m=1.00\,\mathrm{mg}$, $\Delta x=0.100\,\mathrm{mm}$. Find the lower bound on $\Delta v_x$.

(b) Proton · $m=m_p$. For this estimate assume $\Delta x=3.00\,\mathrm{fm}$. Find the lower bound on $\Delta v_x$.

Self-calculation complete. Compare the two scales. The full solutions are available once the lecturer announces the in-class code.

The complete solution is released after in-class practice.

Show complete solution · R2

$\Delta x\,\Delta p_x\ge h/(4\pi)$ and $\Delta p_x=m\Delta v_x$ give a lower bound $h/(4\pi m\Delta x)$, not a prediction of the actual uncertainty.

(a) $1.00\,\mathrm{mg}=1.00\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $0.100\,\mathrm{mm}=1.00\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m$.

$$\frac{6.626\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}}{4\pi(1.00\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{kg})(1.00\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m)} =5.27\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.$$

(b) With $3.00\,\mathrm{fm}=3.00\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm m$:

$$\frac{6.626\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}}{4\pi(1.673\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg})(3.00\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm m)} =1.05\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.$$

The first bound is negligible in this context. The second is about $3.5\%$ of the speed of light.

H1 · Zero-point energy in a rectangular 3D box

Theory · Particle in a 3D box

See how each edge contributes to the ground-state energy, then try the calculation with the lengths below. L22, slides 21–22 and PDF p. 84 (Exercise 7a–b).

Find the zero-point energy of an electron in a rectangular box with edge lengths $1.00\,\mathrm{nm}$, $2.00\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $3.00\,\mathrm{nm}$.

Enter the ground-state energy.

The complete solution is released with the homework code.

Show complete solution · H1

Set $n_x=n_y=n_z=1$. The sum of inverse squared edge lengths is $(1+1/4+1/9)\,\mathrm{nm^{-2}}=1.3611\times10^{18}\,\mathrm{m^{-2}}$.

$$E_{1,1,1}=\frac{h^2}{8m_e}\left(\frac{1}{L_x^2}+\frac{1}{L_y^2}+\frac{1}{L_z^2}\right)=8.20\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm J.$$

As a check, the 3D result must be greater than the contribution of the shortest edge alone.
