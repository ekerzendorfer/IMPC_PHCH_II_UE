1 · Nuclides, alpha decay and beta decay

A nuclide is identified by its mass number $A$ (protons plus neutrons) and atomic number $Z$ (protons). Its neutron number is $N=A-Z$. The chemical element is fixed by $Z$, not by its mass number.

$$ {}^{A}_{Z}X\qquad N=A-Z. $$

Alpha (α) emission

The nucleus emits a helium-4 nucleus containing two protons and two neutrons. The daughter has $A'=A-4$ and $Z'=Z-2$.

$$ {}^{A}_{Z}X\to{}^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}. $$

Beta-minus (β⁻) emission

A neutron changes into a proton while an electron and an electron antineutrino are emitted. The daughter has $A'=A$ and $Z'=Z+1$.

$$ {}^{A}_{Z}X\to{}^{A}_{Z+1}Y+e^-+\bar\nu_e. $$

At a more microscopic level the proton is uud and the neutron udd. In beta-minus decay a down quark converts into an up quark through the weak interaction. You do not need to calculate weak-interaction processes in this unit.

2 · Nuclear mass changes and reaction energy

Important distinction: The binding-energy mass defect compares a bound nucleus with its separate constituents. The reaction mass difference compares all initial particles with all products. Both use mass–energy equivalence, but their reference systems differ.

For a reaction with a common mass convention on both sides:

$$Q=\left(\sum m_{\mathrm{initial}}-\sum m_{\mathrm{final}}\right)c^2.$$

If $Q>0$, energy is released (an exoergic reaction). $Q$ is the total energy available from the reaction; the share received by any one product is determined by conservation of momentum and energy.

Choosing masses correctly. Atomic masses include electrons. For the alpha decay of a neutral atom, and for the D–T reaction using neutral hydrogen and helium atoms, the number of atomic electrons is the same on each side. Therefore the tabulated atomic masses can be used consistently at the accuracy required here. Do not mix bare nuclear masses with neutral atomic masses without accounting for electrons.

Unit conversions:

$$ 1\,\mathrm u = 1.66053906660\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg},\quad 1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J. $$ $$ 1\,\mathrm u\,c^2\approx931.494\,\mathrm{MeV}. $$

Worked method with symbolic data: Compute total mass on each side, subtract final from initial, check the sign, and multiply by the mass–energy factor. If requested in joules, convert u to kilograms before multiplying by $c^2$; if requested in MeV, the shortcut avoids unnecessary intermediate rounding.

Where does binding energy enter?

With consistent atomic masses, the total binding energy of a nuclide can be approximated using its neutral atomic mass $m_{\mathrm{atom}}$, the neutral hydrogen-atom mass $m({}^1\mathrm H)$ and neutron mass $m_n$:

$$ B\approx\left[Z\,m({}^1\mathrm H)+N\,m_n-m_{\mathrm{atom}}\right]c^2.$$

The small atomic-electron binding correction is neglected at this level. The binding energy per nucleon, $B/A$, is useful for understanding the overall trends of nuclear stability.

3 · Why can alpha decay and fusion both release energy?

Both processes transform mass into kinetic energy and other forms of energy when their total product mass is smaller than their total starting mass. But the mechanisms are fundamentally different.

Alpha decay · unstable heavy nucleus

A heavy nucleus emits a tightly bound helium-4 nucleus. This is a spontaneous radioactive process; its probability per unit time is characterised by the decay constant. The heavy daughter nucleus recoils.

It is not the same as splitting a nucleus into two comparable heavy fragments (nuclear fission).

Deuterium–tritium fusion · light nuclei combine

Two positively charged nuclei must approach to nuclear distances despite electrostatic repulsion. This requires very energetic conditions or quantum tunnelling. The result includes a helium-4 nucleus and a fast neutron.

In a fusion device, most of the D–T reaction energy is carried by the neutron; an alpha particle can help heat the plasma.

Energy per event is not power. Reactor power additionally depends on how many reactions occur each second, what fraction of their energy is captured, and the efficiency of conversion to electricity. Fusion, spontaneous decay and induced fission must not be conflated.

Applications: Radioisotope thermoelectric generators exploit the heat from certain radioactive decays; today's commercial nuclear power reactors use induced fission; controlled fusion for net electricity production is a separate engineering challenge. Natural stellar fusion is yet another context (and the Sun is not a D–T reactor).

Reflect after R1: Both G1 and R1 produce helium-4. Why does one happen spontaneously, while the other needs fusion conditions? What would you need to know to convert a reaction Q-value into electrical power?

4 · Hydrogen energy levels and Balmer lines

In the simple Bohr/Coulomb model, the bound hydrogen energy levels are discrete and negative. The ionisation limit is chosen as zero energy. The energy depends on the principal quantum number $n$:

$$E_n=-\frac{hc\,\widetilde R_{\mathrm H}}{n^2}.$$

For emission $n_i>n_f$. The atom loses energy, while the photon receives positive energy $E_\gamma=E_{n_i}-E_{n_f}=hc/\lambda$.

Using $\nu=c/\lambda$, the Rydberg relation for vacuum wavelengths follows:

$$\frac1\lambda=\widetilde R_{\mathrm H}\left(\frac1{n_f^2}-\frac1{n_i^2}\right).$$

Unit warning: $\widetilde R_{\mathrm H}$ is a wavenumber in m⁻¹, not the gas constant $R$. Some spectra are reported as air wavelengths rather than vacuum wavelengths. For high precision, distinguish the hydrogen reduced-mass Rydberg constant from the infinite-nuclear-mass value; this worksheet uses rounded input or explicitly specified constants.

5 · Exponential decay, activity and half-life

A radioactive decay is a stochastic nuclear event. For a large population of the same radionuclide, its expected number of remaining nuclei decreases exponentially. Activity is the number of decays per second and is proportional to the number of radioactive nuclei when the decay constant is fixed.

$$N_t=N_0e^{-\lambda t},\qquad A_{\mathrm{act},t}=\lambda N_t,\qquad t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}.$$

Symbol warning: $A_{\mathrm{act}}$ denotes activity, in becquerels (s⁻¹). It is not the same as the mass number $A$ in a nuclide symbol.

Because activity and nucleus count have the same relative decay:

$$\frac{A_{\mathrm{act},t}}{A_{\mathrm{act},0}}=\left(\frac12\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}.$$

Method example (different from R2): After 1.5 half-lives, the expected remaining fraction is $2^{-1.5}\approx0.354$, or about 35.4%. That lies between 50% (one half-life) and 25% (two half-lives).

To determine the elapsed time from a measured fraction $f=A_t/A_0$ instead, take the natural logarithm:

$$t=-\frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln2}\,\ln f.$$

Dating an unknown requires assumptions beyond this equation: the initial amount or reference activity must be known, the relevant material must behave as a sufficiently closed system, and production or contamination must be controlled.
