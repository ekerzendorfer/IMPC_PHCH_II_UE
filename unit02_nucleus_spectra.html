Constants, input notation and working conventions

For nuclear calculations, use neutral atomic masses consistently. A neutron mass is not an atomic mass. In G1/R1, the atomic electron counts balance on both sides; neglected differences in electron binding energies are below the precision needed here.

$c=299\,792\,458\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; $1\;\mathrm u=1.66053906660\times10^{-27}\;\mathrm{kg}$; $1\;\mathrm{eV}=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\;\mathrm J$; $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\;\mathrm{J\,s}$.

Calculation shortcut: $1\;\mathrm u\,c^2\approx931.494\;\mathrm{MeV}$. It gives the same result as converting u → kg → J → eV.

Enter numbers only, in the unit displayed. For scientific notation use 2.5e-12 or 2,5e-12, not multiplication signs or units. Keep extra digits until the final rounding.

G1 · Alpha decay of polonium-210

Theory · Nuclides, alpha and beta decay · Mass and energy

L21, slides 11, 16 and 20; T02 Try-Test, Question 1 (PDF p. 5).

Polonium-210 belongs to the uranium–radium decay series and undergoes alpha decay to lead-206. Write the balanced nuclear equation and calculate the total energy released per decay in MeV from the atomic masses.

Neutral atomMass
²¹⁰Po209.98287 u
²⁰⁶Pb205.97446 u
⁴He4.00260 u

Before calculating: Where is the alpha particle in the balanced equation? Does the total mass of the final products increase or decrease?

Step 1 · Balance the nuclear equation
$$ {}^{210}_{84}\mathrm{Po}\longrightarrow{}^{206}_{82}\mathrm{Pb}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}. $$

The sums of both mass numbers and nuclear charges are equal: 206 + 4 = 210; 82 + 2 = 84.

Step 2 · Find the mass difference
$$\Delta m=m(^{210}\mathrm{Po})-\left[m(^{206}\mathrm{Pb})+m(^{4}\mathrm{He})\right]=\boxed{0.00581\;\mathrm u}.$$

The positive mass difference represents energy released to the products, primarily as their kinetic energy.

Step 3 · Convert the mass defect into energy
$$Q=\Delta m\,c^2=0.00581\;\mathrm u\times931.494\;\frac{\mathrm{MeV}}{\mathrm u} =\boxed{5.41\;\mathrm{MeV}}.$$

Alternatively convert u into kg, calculate in joules and divide by the energy corresponding to one eV. Important: 5.41 MeV is the total decay energy, not exactly the alpha particle's kinetic energy; the daughter nucleus recoils.

Extension: Why can both decay and fusion release energy? · Compare this result later with R1.

Guided · 11 min

G2 · The hydrogen Hγ line

Theory · Hydrogen levels and spectral lines

L22, slides 4–7; Q02, 3rd Repetition, Question 1 (PDF p. 8); L25, Exercise 2 (precision extension).

The blue-violet hydrogen Hγ line corresponds to the transition $n_i=5$ to $n_f=2$. Treat 434 nm as a rounded vacuum wavelength. Determine the emitted photon energy in eV and estimate the hydrogen Rydberg wavenumber.

Before calculating: Does the atom gain or lose energy when it emits a photon? Why do we know which level is higher?

Step 1 · Energy of the emitted photon
$$\lambda=434\times10^{-9}\;\mathrm m,\qquad E_\gamma=\frac{hc}{\lambda}=\boxed{2.86\;\mathrm{eV}}.$$

Photon energy is positive; the atom's energy changes by $E_f-E_i=-E_\gamma$.

Step 2 · Use the Rydberg relation
$$\frac1\lambda=\widetilde R_{\mathrm H}\left(\frac1{n_f^2}-\frac1{n_i^2}\right),\qquad \widetilde R_{\mathrm H}=\frac{1}{434\times10^{-9}\,(1/2^2-1/5^2)}\approx\boxed{1.10\times10^7\;\mathrm{m^{-1}}}.$$

The given wavelength has only three significant figures. Do not report six significant figures for a constant deduced from 434 nm.

C1 · Complete two nuclear transformations

Theory · N, Z and nuclear equations

Find the missing daughter nuclide in each reaction. Enter its mass number A and atomic number Z; then write its chemical symbol on paper.

(a) Alpha decay ·
$$ {}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra}\longrightarrow{}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}+{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} $$

(b) Beta-minus decay ·
$$ {}^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th}\longrightarrow{}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}+{}^{0}_{-1}e+\bar\nu_e $$

The complete explanation becomes available with the in-class code.

Show complete solution · C1

(a) $^{222}_{86}\mathrm{Rn}$: alpha emission removes two protons and two neutrons, so $A$ decreases by four and $Z$ by two.

(b) $^{234}_{91}\mathrm{Pa}$: in β⁻ decay a neutron turns into a proton, an electron and an electron antineutrino, so $A$ stays the same and $Z$ increases by one.

C2 · Quarks inside nucleons

Theory · Protons and neutrons

L21, slides 6–8.

A proton contains uud. Which combination belongs to a neutron, and how does a nucleon change during β⁻ decay?

The full explanation is released after practice.

Show explanation · C2

The proton is uud and the neutron is udd. In β⁻ decay a neutron converts into a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. At the quark level, one down quark changes into an up quark through the weak interaction.

(a) Enter the total mass difference Δm = mass before − mass after.

(b) Calculate the total released energy (Q-value).

Compare with G1 — a question about usable nuclear energy. Both reactions release a helium-4 nucleus, yet one is radioactive decay and the other is fusion. What makes their energy releases possible? Why does fusion need suitable conditions, while polonium decays spontaneously? Does an energy value in MeV already tell us the power of a reactor?

Optional transfer · pJ and energy carriers

Convert the total energy of one fusion event into pJ. Without calculating the energy split yet, predict which product might carry more kinetic energy: the neutron or the helium nucleus. What might each particle do in a fusion device? The detailed energy split is discussed with the released solution.

The complete calculation and interpretation will be unlocked after practice.

Show complete solution · R1

The mass of the two starting atoms is $2.014101778+3.016049282=5.030151060\;\mathrm u$. The products have $4.002603254+1.008664916=5.011268170\;\mathrm u$.

$$\Delta m=5.030151060-5.011268170=\boxed{0.018882890\;\mathrm u}.$$ $$Q=\Delta m\,c^2=(0.018882890\;\mathrm u)\left(931.494\;\frac{\mathrm{MeV}}{\mathrm u}\right) \approx\boxed{17.59\;\mathrm{MeV}}.$$

For one reaction, $Q\approx2.82\;\mathrm{pJ}$. Fusion and alpha decay both release energy because the products have a lower total mass-energy, but the reaction mechanism and practical conditions are different. Fusion of positively charged nuclei requires overcoming or tunnelling through the Coulomb barrier; a radioactive alpha decay proceeds spontaneously for the unstable parent nucleus. Useful power also depends on reaction rate and energy conversion.

The energy is shared between the helium nucleus (approximately 3.5 MeV) and the neutron (approximately 14.1 MeV). This is different from claiming that all 17.59 MeV heats the plasma directly.

R2 · How much iodine-131 activity remains?

Theory · First-order decay and half-life

L21, slides 23 and 50.

Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8.0 days. What percentage of its initial activity remains after 20 days? Assume no replenishment of the isotope. Show your reasoning on paper.

(a) How many half-lives have passed?

(b) Enter the remaining activity as a percentage of the initial value.

Optional exploration · Half-Life Hourglass

Open Half-Life Hourglass ↗. Compare the graph after 1, 2 and 3 half-lives. Your result at 2.5 half-lives must lie between the values for 2 and 3. Model limits and suggested questions.

The complete solution will be released after practice.

Show complete solution · R2

After 20 days, the number of elapsed half-lives is $20/8.0=2.5$. Activity $A=\lambda N$ decreases by the same fraction as the number of radioactive nuclei, as long as the radionuclide and its decay constant are unchanged.

$$\frac{A_t}{A_0}=\left(\frac12\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}=2^{-2.5}=0.1767767\ldots \quad\Rightarrow\quad\boxed{17.7\%}.$$

Plausibility: two half-lives leave 25%; three leave 12.5%. The answer must lie between them.

H1 · Date a sample with caesium-137

Theory · Reversing the decay law

T02, Question 2 (PDF p. 6).

Caesium-137 has a half-life of 30.2 years. A sample has 35.2% of its initial activity remaining. Calculate the sample's age in years. Why must the age lie between one and two half-lives?

Optional solution released separately.

Show complete solution · H1
$$t=\frac{t_{1/2}}{\ln2}\ln\left(\frac{A_0}{A_t}\right) =\frac{30.2\;\mathrm{years}}{\ln2}\ln\left(\frac1{0.352}\right) \approx\boxed{45.5\;\mathrm{years}}.$$

The remaining activity is less than 50% (one half-life) but greater than 25% (two half-lives), so the age must be between 30.2 and 60.4 years.

Optional challenge

H2 · The longest-wavelength Balmer line

Theory · The Balmer series

L22, slides 5–7 and appended exercises; L25, Exercise 2.

Calculate the vacuum wavelength of the Hα hydrogen line, $n_i=3\rightarrow n_f=2$, using $\widetilde R_{\mathrm H}=1.09678\times10^{7}\;\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Why is Hα at a longer wavelength than Hγ?

Extra challenge · remove the constant

Determine $\lambda_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{H\gamma}}$ using only the quantum numbers. Why does the Rydberg constant cancel?

Optional solution released separately.

Show complete solution · H2
$$\frac1{\lambda_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}}=\widetilde R_{\mathrm H}\left(\frac1{2^2}-\frac1{3^2}\right) =\widetilde R_{\mathrm H}\frac5{36},\quad \lambda_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}=\frac{36}{5\widetilde R_{\mathrm H}}\approx\boxed{656.5\;\mathrm{nm}}.$$

Hα has a smaller level-energy gap than Hγ, so its photon energy is smaller and its wavelength is longer. The ratio is $\lambda_{\mathrm{H\alpha}}/\lambda_{\mathrm{H\gamma}}=(1/4-1/5^2)/(1/4-1/3^2)=1.512$, with the common Rydberg factor cancelling.
